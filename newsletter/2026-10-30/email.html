Victorian Year 12 exams begin with smart glasses and phones banned

EducationHQ (AAP)  ·  28 Oct 2026

More than 50,000 students sat the first of more than 160 final-year exams across 584 centres, and schools and supervisors were advised of rules banning smart glasses, mobile phones and other electronic devices. La Trobe University's Therese Keane said exam procedures need to keep pace as devices become "smaller, smarter and less visible".

Why it matters: Wearables are now named in exam rules, so it is worth checking what your own school's assessment rules say about them and what students have been told.

AI tutor matches expert humans in the moment, but test scores don't move

The 74  ·  29 Oct 2026

In a randomised trial of 1,179 students in 10 UK public schools, Eedi and Google DeepMind found an AI tutor matched expert human tutors at helping students fix mistakes during sessions. After 10 weeks, though, neither kind of tutoring moved scores on a broad standardised maths test, and about one in five students never replied when a tutor popped up.

Why it matters: Success inside a tutoring app is not the same as lasting learning, so ask any vendor for results on an independent test and plan how to engage the students who ignore the prompts.

Most tweens and teens use AI chatbots for schoolwork and emotional support

NPR (Rhitu Chatterjee)  ·  28 Oct 2026

A Jed Foundation survey of more than 5,000 US tweens and teens found 58% use chatbots for schoolwork, and a similar number turn to them when sad, stressed, lonely or overwhelmed. Using chatbots for companionship or when distressed was linked with worse mental health, although the Jed Foundation's Kayla Anderson said it is unclear whether AI use contributes to poor wellbeing or distressed students are more likely to turn to AI.

Why it matters: Students may be using the same chatbot for study and for comfort, so wellbeing conversations and AI-use expectations are worth linking rather than treating separately.

Teachers move learning back into the classroom as AI spreads

Reuters (via Yahoo News)  ·  28 Oct 2026

Reuters spoke with a dozen US teachers and education experts and found educators assigning less take-home work, increasing in-class writing and discussion, and redesigning tasks to measure thinking rather than answers. San Francisco sixth-grade teacher Tristen Chang replaced take-home essays with oral exams of 15 questions that students can answer in as little as five minutes.

Why it matters: Short oral check-ins and live demonstrations, such as recording a chat show with character hot-seating to show understanding of a novel, are low-tech ways to see what a student actually knows.

Common Sense census: most teens use AI, but few enjoy it a lot

The National News Desk (Common Sense Media census)  ·  27 Oct 2026

Common Sense Media's first broad survey of tween and teen tech use in five years (ages 9 to 17) found 77% of teens use AI in their free time, but just 11% of those users enjoy it a lot. About half of tweens also use AI, even though Common Sense Media says the platforms are not meant for kids that young.

Why it matters: Upper-primary students may already be using AI outside school, so conversations about safe and sensible use need not wait until high school.
